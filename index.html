Big picture

What a protist is, and why the group is so messy

A protist is any member of a diverse group of eukaryotic, predominantly unicellular, microscopic organisms that is not a plant, animal, or fungus.

That definition is really an exclusion: "protist" collects every eukaryote left over after plants, animals and fungi are removed. Because of that, protists do not share one ancestor that excludes everything else. Some are closer to plants (red and green algae), some are closer to animals and fungi (choanoflagellates, nucleariids). This is why the lecture organizes them into six supergroups based on shared ancestry, and why every group is defined by a structural signature (a groove, sacs under the membrane, a kind of pseudopod, a kind of flagellum) instead of by lifestyle.

General characteristics (slide 6)
  • Mostly aquatic
  • Mostly unicellular, a few multicellular (kelp, some red and green algae)
  • Have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (eukaryotic)
  • Autotrophic, heterotrophic, or mixotrophic
  • Symbiotic, parasitic, or free-living
  • Move by pseudopodia, cilia, or flagella
  • Reproduce sexually or asexually
Terms to get right
  • Autotroph: makes its own food (photosynthesis).
  • Heterotroph: takes in organic food (ingests or absorbs).
  • Mixotroph: does both. Euglena photosynthesizes in light and absorbs food in the dark.
  • Testate / atestate: with or without a shell (test).
  • Pellicle: flexible protein layer under the membrane that keeps shape.
Learning objectives
  • Define protists and classify them into major groups
  • Identify key characteristics of each group
  • Describe their structural and functional diversity
  • Explain their ecological roles and significance
Mechanism

Endosymbiosis: count the membranes

Chloroplasts came from swallowed photosynthesizers. How many times that swallowing happened tells you which supergroup you are in, and you can read it off the number of membranes around the chloroplast.

Why it works: every engulfment wraps the prey in the predator's membrane. Cyanobacterium has 2 membranes of its own, which stay as the chloroplast's double membrane. Swallow a whole alga and you add the alga's outer membrane plus the host's food-vacuole membrane, so the count climbs to 4. Membrane counting is evidence about history, the same way rock layers are.

Classification (slide 7)

The six supergroups

Tap a column to open that supergroup. Groups sitting next to each other on a branch share a closer ancestor. Note where fungi and animals end up: inside Opisthokonta, next to protists.

Connection to newer taxonomy: the slide on Rhizaria says it belongs to the SAR clade (Stramenopiles + Alveolata + Rhizaria). Evidence from genetic studies suggests Stramenopiles and Alveolates group with Rhizaria, so many current textbooks drop "Chromalveolata" and use SAR instead. For your exam, use the lecture's six names; just know why "SAR" shows up.

Outline parts 3–5

Ecological roles, reproduction, and human health

The lecture outline lists these topics. Items marked beyond slides are standard textbook context added to connect the ideas; the rest come straight from the slides.

Ecological roles

  • Primary producers: diatoms, dinoflagellates, red and green algae, glaucophytes (freshwater). Marine phytoplankton are estimated to carry out roughly half of Earth's photosynthesis beyond slides
  • Decomposers: oomycetes (water molds), slime molds
  • Reef building: red algae deposit CaCO₃
  • Nutrient cycling: red algae; radiolarians and diatoms in the silicon cycle
  • Food webs: radiolarians, ciliates and forams eat bacteria and smaller protists
  • Records of the past: foram tests act as biostratigraphic and paleoclimate markers
  • Symbionts: parabasalids in termite guts digest wood beyond slides

Reproduction & life cycles

  • Binary fission (asexual): diplomonads, cercozoa, Entamoeba trophozoites
  • Asexual: radiolaria
  • Complex life cycle: foraminifera
  • Entamoeba: cyst → excystation → trophozoite → encystation
  • Plasmodial slime mold (sexual): haploid spores → amoeboid or flagellated cells → fusion (plasmogamy, then karyogamy) → diploid zygote → multinucleate plasmodium → sporangia → meiosis → haploid spores
  • Cellular slime mold: single amoebae aggregate → mound → migrating slug → fruiting body → spores
  • Fungi: reproduce via spores

Human health (PH lens)

  • Entamoeba histolytica: amoebic dysentery and liver abscess. Spread fecal-orally through contaminated food and water, so it matters after floods and in areas without safe water and sanitation
  • Plasmodium (Apicomplexa): malaria. Under the DOH malaria elimination program, most remaining local cases in recent years have come from Palawan beyond slides
  • Dinoflagellates: red tides cause paralytic shellfish poisoning; BFAR issues shellfish bans beyond slides
  • Giardia (diplomonad), Trichomonas (parabasalid), Trypanosoma (kinetoplastid) beyond slides
  • Carrageenan from red algae: the Philippines is one of the world's major producers of carrageenan seaweed

Shape of the pseudopod

Four pseudopod types

LoboseBlunt, rounded lobes. Amoebozoa (gymnamoebas, Entamoeba).
FiloseThin, threadlike. Cercozoa (also nucleariids).
Reticulose (reticulopodia)Branching, fused into a net. Foraminifera.
AxopodiaStiff, straight spokes supported by microtubules. Radiolaria.

Why the shape matters: blunt lobes are good for crawling and engulfing on a surface, which fits gut and soil amoebae. Thin threads and nets increase surface area, so floating rhizarians can snag prey from open water. That is why lobose = Amoebozoa and thin = Rhizaria is the first split to make.

Teach-back prompts (answer out loud, then check)
  1. Why do apicomplexans, which are parasites, sit next to photosynthetic dinoflagellates?
    CheckThey share alveoli under the membrane, so they share ancestry. Evidence suggests apicomplexans even keep a remnant plastid (the apicoplast), a leftover of photosynthetic ancestors.
  2. Why are kelp and red seaweed in different supergroups if both are "seaweed"?
    CheckKelp (brown alga) got its plastid by secondary endosymbiosis and has hairy flagella in its reproductive cells, so it is a stramenopile. Red algae are primary endosymbionts in Archaeplastida. Body form converged; ancestry did not.
  3. Why are diplomonads and parabasalids missing normal mitochondria?
    CheckThey live in low-oxygen environments like guts, where aerobic respiration is useless. Evidence suggests they reduced their mitochondria to small remnants rather than never having them.
  4. Why is the cyst, not the trophozoite, the infective stage of Entamoeba?
    CheckThe cyst wall survives outside the body and through stomach acid; the trophozoite dies outside the host. So cysts carry the infection from stool to water to the next person.
  5. Why are fungi in a protist lecture at all?
    CheckBecause ancestry places fungi (with nucleariids) and animals (with choanoflagellates) inside Opisthokonta. They share a single posterior flagellum on motile cells.
